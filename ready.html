Before day one

Get ready

You got a yes. Now four things need to be lined up before you start: a work permit, your papers, a way to get paid, and a ride. Last read from official pages on Oct 9, 2026. The permit and ride sections are for California. In Georgia, New York or Illinois, see the rules for your state; the papers and pay sections apply everywhere in the US.

This is a summary, not legal or tax advice. Each section links to the page we read. Schools, transit agencies and employers can change their rules, so check the link before you rely on a date or a price. Your checklist below is saved on this device only.

0 of 0 done

    1. Work permit

    If you are under 18 and have a paid job at a business, California requires a work permit. You get the job first, then your school issues the permit. The employer and a parent also sign. The full rules on hours and ages are on the Rules page.

    Pick a district to see its steps. If yours is not listed, ask your school’s main office or attendance office. They issue permits during the school year, and in most districts the steps are the same: get hired, fill out the form with your employer and a parent, hand it in, wait a few days.

    2. Your papers

    On your first day you fill out a federal form called Form I-9. It proves who you are and that you can work in the US. You show original documents, within three business days of starting. Photocopies do not count, except a certified copy of a birth certificate.

    You choose which documents

    The rule is: one document from List A, or one from List B plus one from List C. The official instructions say employers must let you choose which acceptable document to show. They cannot tell you which one.

    Many teens use a school ID with a photo (List B) plus an unrestricted Social Security card or a birth certificate with an official seal (List C). All three are on the official lists. A passport alone also works (List A).

    Under 18 with no photo ID?

    For people under 18 who cannot show another List B document, the list allows a school record or report card, a clinic, doctor or hospital record, or a day-care record in place of ID. You still need a List C document. If your employer uses E-Verify, the rules are stricter, so ask them before your first day.

    Source: USCIS acceptable documents and the M-274 handbook.

    Lost your Social Security card?

    A replacement is free. The Social Security Administration says you may not need a card if you know your number, but for Form I-9 you need an acceptable List C document, so a birth certificate can work instead. A replacement card arrives in 5 to 10 business days after your request is complete. Call 1-800-772-1213 to ask what you need. The page does not say whether a teen under 18 can apply online.

    Source: SSA, replace your card.

    Need a California birth certificate?

    The state charges $31 a copy (since Jan 1, 2026) and says the average wait is 4 to 6 weeks, plus mail time. The county office where you were born is often faster. An “informational” copy cannot be used to prove your identity, so order an “authorized” copy, which needs a notarized form. Start early. A parent can help you order it.

    Source: CDPH fees, processing times.

    3. Getting paid

    A place to put the money

    The FDIC says minors can open a custodial account (you own it, an adult manages it) or a joint account with an adult, and may be able to open an account on their own, depending on the bank and state law. It also says that under 18, an adult may need to open the account with you. Only open a joint account with an adult you trust: both of you can take the money out.

    Sources: FDIC, money management for youth; FDIC Money Smart guide. Call a bank and ask what they offer for your age.

    How and when you get paid

    California employers must pay at least twice a month on regular paydays set in advance. Ask on day one: when is payday, and is it a check or direct deposit? A California Labor Standards booklet says an employer can pay by direct deposit only if you agree to it. If pay is late or missing, see If something goes wrong.

    Sources: DLSE paydays FAQ; the direct-deposit rule comes from a 2013 DLSE booklet, so confirm with the Labor Commissioner at 833-526-4636.

    Two forms about taxes

    You will fill out the federal Form W-4 and the California DE 4. They tell your employer how much income tax to hold back from each check. If you do not give a DE 4, the employer must use “Single” with zero allowances. Ask a parent to sit with you.

    The W-4 lets you claim an exemption only if you had no federal income tax liability last year and expect none this year. The form warns that you could then owe taxes and penalties. Check with a parent before you do.

    Sources: IRS Form W-4; California DE 4. Whether you must file a tax return depends on your income: use the IRS “Check if you need to file” page with a parent.

    What you will see on a paystub

    Most paystubs show lines for federal income tax, California income tax, Social Security (6.2% of pay for the employee), Medicare (1.45%) and California SDI (1.3% in 2026, on all wages). SDI pays benefits if a worker cannot work because of illness, injury or pregnancy, and it also funds Paid Family Leave. Federal and state income tax withheld may come back as a refund when a return is filed.

    Sources: EDD rates; IRS Topic 751; FTB.

    4. A ride to work

    A free or cheap bus pass can decide whether a job works. Each agency sets its own youth rules. The shared Clipper card is the way to get most youth discounts in the Bay Area. Pick the place you live.

    Applying to a city summer program, or missing out

    City programs like New York’s Summer Youth Employment Program, Chicago Youth Works or Atlanta’s summer jobs are the biggest paid chances for many teens, and they use online portals and lotteries. Studies of these programs show what trips teens up.

    Get your papers ready before the portal opens

    In Boston’s 2022 summer jobs portal, about one third of teens who made a profile never finished a valid application, and onboarding took about 25 days on average with 10 steps, including a report card, proof of address and a Social Security card. Gather those now: a photo ID or school ID, your Social Security card or number, a proof of address (a bill or school letter), your latest report card and a parent who can sign. Many portals open for only a few weeks.

    Source: Northeastern Dukakis Center, Boston SuccessLink, 2022 data. One city, mostly low-income teens.

    Apply to more than one

    In that Boston study, 53% of teens applied to only one job and the average was about three. If a program lets you apply to several jobs or sites, do it. A teen who applies broadly has more chances than a teen who applies once.

    Same source. The authors note that some one-job applicants already knew the employer.

    If you do not get in

    When New York ran its lottery, most teens who lost, especially the 14 and 15 year olds, did not find another job that summer. Do not wait for next year’s lottery. Use the programs you can apply to any time, volunteer roles that build a reference, and the Playbook to ask a person you know.

    Source: MDRC, New York City SYEP evaluation.

    Watch for fake-check job scams

    Among 18 to 24 year olds who reported scams to the Better Business Bureau, employment scams had the highest median loss, $1,819, and about 43% of the employment-scam reports mentioned fake checks. A real job does not send you a check and ask you to send money back or buy equipment. See the scam checklist.

    Source: BBB Institute, 2023. Ages 18 to 24 who chose to report, not all teens.
