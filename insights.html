    What the research says about first jobs

    Most of the strong studies are about big programs for low-income city teens, so each card says who was studied. That is not the same as you finding a job on your own. Where we could not find the original study, we left the claim out.

    Pay

    A summer job pays that year

    Teens who won New York City’s summer job lottery ended the year about $876 ahead after counting other earnings they gave up.

    Who: about 294,000 lottery applicants ages 14 to 21 in New York City, 2005 to 2008. Source: Gelber, Isen and Kessler.

    Careful

    Do not count on it to raise later pay

    The same study found about $100 a year less in earnings over the next three years, and no change in college enrollment. A first job helps you in other ways: people who know you, a reference, and proof you show up.

    Same study as above.

    Programs

    Mentored job programs can change a lot

    In Chicago, an 8-week job with an adult mentor led to 43% fewer violent-crime arrests over 16 months. In Boston, winners graduated on time 4.4 points more often (63.4% in the comparison group). Chicago found no school gain, so it is not a rule.

    Who: low-income city teens in organized programs, not ordinary jobs. Sources: Heller, Science 2014; Modestino and Paulsen.

    School year

    Keep school-year hours under 20

    In a national study of students, those working over 20 hours a week did worse in school than those who worked less. This is a link, not proof, because students who choose long hours may differ in other ways.

    Source: Staff, Schulenberg and Bachman, 2010.

    Work in school

    A school program with real work built in

    Students in Career Academies earned 11% more per year for the next 8 years, mostly young men. It had no effect on diplomas. Ask your counselor about career pathway classes, work experience credit and Regional Occupational programs.

    Who: students at 9 mostly urban high schools in a randomized study. Source: MDRC, 2008.

    Normal

    If you do not have a job yet, you are with most teens

    On average in June and July 2026, 35.5% of US 16 to 19 year olds were employed, up from 33.8% in summer 2025. So far that summer it was about 24% for 16 and 17 year olds and about 48% for 18 and 19 year olds. In summer 2000 it was 51.7%.

    National numbers, not California. Source: Pew Research Center, from BLS data.

    People

    Referrals are rare and powerful

    In one hiring dataset, referrals were 1.64% of applications but 17.09% of hires.

    Who: employers using one hiring system, mostly office jobs, data through December 2024. It shows what happens, not that asking causes an offer. Source: Gem 2025 Benchmarks Report.

    People

    People you know a little can matter most

    In experiments on more than 20 million LinkedIn members, acquaintances created the most job moves. In less digital industries, closer ties mattered more, which is closer to a local job for a teen.

    Who: adult professionals, not teens. Source: Rajkumar and others, Science 2022.

    Adults

    An adult who knows you helps, but it is no magic raise

    A review of 30 studies found that having a natural mentor, like a teacher, coach, relative or religious leader, goes with better results for young people. The effect is small, and how close and caring the relationship is matters about twice as much as simply having one. In one national study, a mentor alone did not significantly change later pay.

    Sources: Van Dam and others, 2018; Timpe and Lunkenheimer, 2015. These show a link, not proof.

    Normal

    About half of students have three adults who support them

    52% of students in grades 6 to 12 in one large survey said three or more adults other than their parents support them. So about half do not. That is why the Playbook starts with a people map.

    Who: 121,157 students in communities that ran the survey, 2012 to 2015, so it is not a random sample. Source: Search Institute.

    Employers

    Work ethic matters most

    80.3% of employers said professionalism and work ethic (on time, reliable, works well with others) is very important for high school hires. 70.3% said new high school hires fell short on it.

    Who: 431 employers in 2006, with a 4.8% response rate, so treat it as old and rough. Source: Are They Really Ready to Work?

    What we could not find. No study we could read measures what a typical Northern California teen gets from an ordinary first job. We also left out popular claims, like “working teens earn 16% more later,” because we could not open the original study. We found no good national number on how many applications a teen needs, so we do not quote one.

    How much can you trust these numbers?

    Counts are not quality. A bigger bar means more listed programs, not better ones. Programs repeat across areas when they serve more than one. Always confirm dates on the program’s own site.
