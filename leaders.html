For youth leaders

Run a Career Connections Night

Most teens never ask a grown-up for help finding work, and in one large survey about half of students did not have three adults outside their family who support them. A youth group can fix that in one evening. This kit works for a church, a club, a classroom or a scout troop.

Read this first. This is an unofficial, free kit. It is not legal advice and it does not replace your group’s own child-protection policy, your insurer’s rules or your denomination’s requirements. Where they differ from this page, follow them. This site never collects names or contact details for teens or adults. The fill-in tools below stay in your browser and the paper forms stay with you.

Safety first

The design rule for this kit: groups, not one-on-one. Adults and teens meet in small tables in one room, with other adults in sight. The guide does not match teens with individual adults and nobody should use it to start private messages. Here is what the sources we read say, and where to check.

Use your own policy

Start with your group’s written child-protection policy. Ask your insurer or denomination what they require for events with outside adults. Examples we read: a ministry insurer, Brotherhood Mutual, recommends two screened, unrelated adults at every function and in each room, and prior written parent consent for any one-on-one meeting. That is an insurer’s recommendation, not a California law.

Keep groups small

The MENTOR national guidelines for group mentoring suggest 3 to 6 young people per adult and a total group of about 12 to 15 at most. They are written for ongoing programs, so treat them as a planning guide for one evening. Keep other staff nearby during table time.

MENTOR, Elements of Effective Practice, 5th edition

Guests who are not screened

Many adults you invite will not have been through your screening. One Northern California diocese’s policy lets one-time guest speakers visit only if a screened adult stays with them for the whole visit. Do the same: every guest is greeted, seated and accompanied by a screened adult from your group. Use the briefing sheet below.

Example only: Diocese of San Jose safe environment handbook. It is a Catholic diocese’s rule, not a standard for everyone.

Messages go to the group

After the night, teens send follow-up notes through a parent or your group’s channel. Avoid private messages between adults and teens. Insurer guidance suggests group messages, including parents, and more than one staff member having access to the account.

Brotherhood Mutual on digital communication.

Reporting abuse in California

California treats clergy as mandated reporters. The state’s summary says to report immediately or as soon as practicably possible by phone, with a written report within 36 hours, and not to investigate on your own. Volunteers who work directly with children are generally not mandated reporters but are encouraged to take training. Whether your group counts as a “youth organization” under California law is a question for your attorney or denomination, so we do not say yes or no.

California OCAP summary · HHS Children’s Bureau, clergy in California · state training page. Outside California, ask your state’s child welfare agency.

Teens who help

If teens help run the night (greeters, timekeepers, hosts), they work under a screened adult. Ask their parents. Peer helpers have no criminal record to check, so MENTOR says to lean on references from adults who know them.

Pastor, leader or board: the questions this kit cannot answer for you are whether your group needs background checks for the adults at the table, whether your insurer requires anything for outside guests, and what your state requires for reporting. Ask them before you invite anyone.

The Career Connections Night

One evening. 90 minutes. Teens sit in small groups and each table has one adult who tells what they do and how they started. Teens rotate. The goal is not a job that night. It is a teen who knows what to ask, has met a few adults, and sends one thank-you or follow-up message.

Rotation length and group size are planning suggestions. Examples we read use 5 to 15 minute rotations and 3 to 6 teens per adult. For 24 teens in 4 groups of 6 you need 4 table adults, one host, one floater and one timekeeper. Test the timing with your own group.

4 weeks out

Find the adults

  • Announce the night. Hand out the sign-up sheet to your community.
  • Look for different jobs: nurse, mechanic, teacher, owner of a small shop, someone in tech, a government worker, a tradesperson.
  • Your leaders keep the paper. Do not post names online.
1 week out

Brief everyone

  • Send guests the briefing sheet below. Confirm two days before.
  • Send the note to parents.
  • Teens fill out their people map and write three questions.
Within a week after

Close the loop

  • Every teen sends a thank-you through a parent or your group.
  • Leaders send guests a short thank-you from the group.
  • Use the evaluation answers to fix next time.

The timing for briefing guests a week ahead and confirming two days before comes from a career panel handbook used by Santa Cruz County high schools, which also had every student write three questions and sent thank-you letters within a week. It is an older, school-based example: Career Panels Handbook.

Reference letter scaffold

Teens ask a leader to be a reference. A strong letter says how you know the teen, for how long, and tells one true story about what you saw. Common App’s advice to recommenders is “Anecdotes outshine adjectives,” and the US Labor Department’s CareerOneStop says past employers carry the most weight, and that clergy or counselors count for less unless they are relevant to the job. So the best letters come from someone who saw the teen work: a nursery lead, a sound-booth lead, a shop owner. Only write what you saw. Give the teen a copy.

Do not use pre-written praise. Faith or character statements are for the writer to compose in their own words: PLACEHOLDER FOR PASTOR.

Before you send it: ask the teen and a parent for permission, add your phone or email, and sign it.

A 4-week small-group series

Use this if you want teens to practice before the night, or if you cannot host an evening. Each week is 30 to 45 minutes. The questions are about work, not scripture. Your pastor or leader decides what Bible reading, devotional or prayer goes with each week.

Week 1: Who is around me?

Fill out the people map together. Each teen names five adults and what they do for work. Talk: What job is a mystery to you? Who could you ask?

PLACEHOLDER FOR PASTOR: reading and prayer for this week.

Week 2: Write the ask

Use the message builder to write a five-line message to one adult. Read it aloud to a partner. A leader reads each one before it is sent through a parent.

PLACEHOLDER FOR PASTOR: reading and prayer for this week.

Week 3: Show up well

Practice a 5-minute chat: arrive early, shake hands, ask two questions, say thank you. Read Get ready together: permit, papers, pay and a ride.

Week 4: Follow up

Each teen reports one message sent and one answer received. Plan a 15-minute follow-up. Print the resume and ask a leader to read it. Choose one program from Programs to apply to this month.

Make roles at your group count as real experience

Many teens already serve: sound, slides, nursery, setup, kitchen, greeting, the office. Turn those into something a teen can put on a resume.

  • Give it a title and a supervisor. “Sound booth operator, supervised by [name]” reads better than “helped.”
  • Track hours. A simple log with dates and a signature.
  • Give written feedback twice a year. Two or three sentences with one story. That is the raw material for a reference letter.
  • Let teens teach. A teen who trains a new sound-booth volunteer has leadership to write down.
  • Say thank you in writing. A short note from a leader is a small reference.

Then show the teen how to turn it into resume lines on the resume builder.

What we read, and what we did not

  • MENTOR, Elements of Effective Practice for Mentoring, 5th edition: screening, training and group-size guidance, written for ongoing programs.
  • Brotherhood Mutual, a ministry insurer: supervision ratios and digital communication. Recommendations, not law.
  • California: Office of Child Abuse Prevention summary and clergy as mandated reporters. We read the statutes through a mirror site because the official legislative site blocked our reader, so we did not quote the statute text.
  • CareerOneStop on references and Common App’s tips for recommenders (written for college letters).
  • Research on how adults help young people: see The Numbers.
  • We did not use the Fuller Youth Institute’s Sticky Faith numbers. That research measured faith, not jobs. We also did not find a guide to career nights written for churches, so the run of show above is our own plan built from the sources above, not a tested curriculum.
