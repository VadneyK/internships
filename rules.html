Know your rights

The rules

What the law says about teens, work and pay in California, in plain words. Last read from the official pages on Oct 7, 2026. Georgia, New York or Illinois? See rules in other states. Want the answer for your exact job and age? Use the work permit finder.

This is a summary, not legal advice. Rules change and some details depend on your school district and your job. Each section links to the agency page we read. If your situation is unusual, ask your school’s work permit office or call the California Labor Commissioner at 833-526-4636.

Quick answers

Do I need a work permit?

If you are under 18 and have a paid job at a business, yes. A babysitting or yard-work job for neighbors, or running your own small business, does not need one. Unpaid volunteering at a nonprofit generally does not either.

How young can I work?

Most jobs start at 14. At 12 and 13 you can do things like babysitting, pet care, yard work and newspaper delivery.

What is the pay?

California’s minimum wage is $16.90 an hour in 2026 and rises to $17.40 on Jan 1, 2027. Teens get the same minimum wage as adults. Some cities pay more.

Are unpaid internships legal?

At a nonprofit, school or library, usually yes. At a for-profit company, only if you are the one who mainly benefits, like a class. Otherwise it should be a paid job.

Can I use LinkedIn?

Only at 16 or older. LinkedIn’s own rules say so. Younger students should skip it and use email with a parent’s help.

What if someone asks me to pay to get a job?

Stop. Real employers do not charge you to hire you. See the scam checklist.

Work permit

In California, almost every worker under 18 needs a Permit to Employ and Work for each job, even in summer. Your school issues it. It lists your job, your employer and the hours you may work. The permit is also your proof of age.

Steps

  1. Get the job offer first

    Schools usually will not issue a permit until an employer agrees to hire you.

  2. Fill out the Statement of Intent (form B1-1)

    You, your parent or guardian, and your employer all sign it. Your school may have it online or at the counselor or career office.

  3. Turn it in at your school

    The school checks it and issues the Permit (form B1-4) with the maximum hours you may work. Some districts take a day and some take up to a week.

  4. Give the permit to your employer

    They keep it on file. Permits issued during the school year run out soon after the next school year starts, so renew it each year.

Start one to two weeks before your first shift. Home-schooled, private and charter students go through their own school.

You do not need a permit for

  • Babysitting, lawn mowing, yard work and other occasional odd jobs in private homes
  • Running your own business (tutoring, pet care, selling crafts)
  • Delivering newspapers on your own route
  • Graduates of high school and teens with a certificate of proficiency
  • True unpaid volunteering, with parent permission on file with your school (California regulation 5 CCR 10121)

Working for your parent’s business? You usually still need a permit, and the usual age and hour rules apply, unless it is farm or home work on property the parent controls.

Entertainment jobs (acting, modeling) use a different permit from the Labor Commissioner.

Sources: Permit form B1-4, DIR guide for employers, DIR minors summary charts, DLSE child labor pamphlet (2013 edition; hour tables checked against the newer charts), 5 CCR 10121.

  • When school is out (summer, long breaks), 14 and 15 year olds can work up to 8 hours a day and 40 hours a week. 16 and 17 year olds can work up to 8 hours a day and 48 hours a week.
  • You need to have finished 7th grade to work while school is in session.
  • Your permit can set lower limits than these. Your school can say no or add limits.
  • Breaks: if you work more than 5 hours you get a 30-minute meal break (unpaid), and a paid 10-minute rest break for every 4 hours. You also get a day of rest in seven.
  • Jobs teens cannot do: driving as part of the job, operating forklifts and power saws, roofing, meat slicers and grinders, working in the area of a bar, and more. Under 16, also most construction, warehouse and commercial cooking out of public view, and jobs on ladders.

Sources: DIR minors summary charts, DIR employer guide, Labor Commissioner Youth Advocate brochure. Federal hour rules for 14 and 15 year olds match these (DOL Fact Sheet 43).

Pay

$16.90 an hour in 2026. $17.40 starting Jan 1, 2027.

That is California’s statewide minimum wage, and teens get the same as adults. Fast food workers have a higher state minimum of $20.00. If your city has a higher minimum wage, your employer must pay the higher rate. Ask for the rate before you accept a job.

Known gap: city minimum wages for 2027 are not updated here yet. Issue 7 tracks it, and we will update it in December 2026.

Babysitting and other personal-attendant work are exempt from the state minimum wage, so rates there are up to you and the family. If an employer offers less than minimum wage for a regular job, ask the Labor Commissioner.

Taxes in one minute

  • At a new job you fill out a W-4 form so the employer knows how much tax to hold back. Tips count as income.
  • If you run your own small business (pet care, mowing), you owe self-employment tax once you earn $400 or more in a year.
  • If you had tax held back, you can usually file a tax return to get it back. A parent or tax preparer can help.

Sources: IRS tips for students starting a summer job, IRS Publication 501.

Unpaid internships and volunteering

Usually fine

  • Volunteering at a nonprofit, library, hospital, church, or school program
  • A school-arranged unpaid placement (your counselor can ask about form B1-6)
  • A short shadowing day arranged by a parent or teacher

A red flag

A for-profit company asking you to do real work for free, like answering phones, stocking, or posting on social media, with no teaching and no school link. The federal test (U.S. Department of Labor Fact Sheet 71) asks who mainly benefits, you or the company. If it is the company, you should be paid at least minimum wage, and you need a work permit.

Sources: DOL Fact Sheet 71, 5 CCR 10121.

Contacting adults safely

Reaching out to adults is a skill, and doing it safely is part of the skill.

  • Start with adults a parent knows or who are connected through school, church, a team or a program.
  • Show a parent the message first and copy a parent on early emails to adults you do not know well.
  • Use email or a group thread where a parent can see, rather than private direct messages.
  • Meet where people can see you: a library, an office lobby, a coffee shop, or a video call with a parent nearby. Tell a parent who, where and when.
  • Do not share your home address, school schedule, live location or photos with someone you have not met through a trusted adult.

Red flags: stop and tell a parent

  • An adult asks you to keep your messages secret from your parents.
  • An adult wants to move to a private app or meet alone.
  • Anyone asks for or sends pictures that make you uncomfortable.

If anyone shares or threatens to share an explicit picture of you, tell a trusted adult and report it to the National Center for Missing and Exploited Children (CyberTipline, 1-800-843-5678).

Why these rules: youth organizations like sports leagues, churches and scouting programs commonly use visible, two-adult communication to protect both young people and the adults who work with them. See the FTC guidance on explicit images and the U.S. Center for SafeSport policy. This guide follows the same approach: questions go in public, and nobody asks a student to move to a private chat.

Scam checklist

Job scams are one of the fastest-growing kinds of fraud. The Federal Trade Commission says reported losses from job scams more than tripled from 2020 to 2023, and young adults report losing money to scams more often than older adults.

  1. Did a stranger contact you first?

    Slow down, especially by text, WhatsApp or Telegram about easy online “tasks.”

  2. Does it cost you anything?

    Fees, starter kits, training, certifications, “refundable” deposits. Real employers and the government never charge to hire you.

  3. Did they send you a check or ask you to move money?

    The check will be fake. The bank can take the money back weeks later.

  4. Search the name plus “scam,” “review” or “complaint.”

    Look for a real website, address and phone number.

  5. Show a parent or counselor before you reply.

    A real employer will also know about work permits.

Paying for a “guaranteed internship”?

Some companies sell internship programs to teens for thousands of dollars. Not all are scams, but ask: Who is the real host? Will I do real work, or mostly classes? What is the refund policy? Could I arrange the same thing for free with a parent’s friend, a library or a school program? The programs in this guide are free or are clearly marked with their cost.

If you lose money

Call your bank or payment app right away. Report it at ReportFraud.ftc.gov and to the California Attorney General.

Sources: FTC: job scams, FTC: fake check scams, FTC: top scams of 2024, FTC data spotlight on job scams.

If something goes wrong at work

Not paid, paid too little, or no breaks

Call the California Labor Commissioner at 833-526-4636 (English and Spanish, Monday to Friday, 8 a.m. to 5 p.m.) or email the Youth Advocate Program at YAP@dir.ca.gov. Keep your schedule, hours and pay stubs. You can file a wage claim online.

California labor law protects workers regardless of immigration status, and it is illegal for an employer to punish you for asking about your rights.

Something feels unsafe

Tell a supervisor, then a parent. If you are asked to do a job you are not allowed to do at your age, such as driving, using a slicer or power saw, or working past your permit hours, you can say no and tell your school’s work permit office.

Sources: DIR: how to file a wage claim, Youth Advocate brochure.
