Moves 2 and 3

The playbook

Most teens never ask. You will, in four steps. Everything you type stays on this device.

1. People map 2. Your message 3. Send it 4. After the yes

1. Your people map

Write five adults you already know and what they do for work. Adults you know a little count. A parent’s coworker, someone from church, your coach, the owner of the shop you visit every week. Then press Write to them on any card.

0 of 5 sent

This works like a notebook in your own browser. We never see what you type, and it is gone if you switch phones or clear your browser data. Save the image if you want to keep it.

Stuck? Ask yourself

  • Who does my family call when the car breaks, the sink leaks, or someone gets sick?
  • Who at church works in a job I find interesting?
  • Which teachers or coaches have a side job or a career before teaching?
  • Which shops, offices or clinics do I visit every month?
  • Who has a business, even a small one?

Not sure what they do? That is a good first question to ask.

2. The five-line message

  1. Who you are

    Name, grade, school.

  2. How you know them

    The connection. This line gets your message read.

  3. What you are curious about

    One thing, said plainly.

  4. One small, specific ask

    With a time limit. Fifteen minutes is easy to say yes to.

  5. Thank them, and make it easy to say no

    “Totally fine if you are busy.”

Build yours

Finish this sentence: “I'm curious about ...”
    Open in my email app Open in Messages

    Check the spelling of their name. Read it out loud once. If you are under 18, show a parent or another trusted adult before you send it.

    3. Send it

    Pick a person and a day

    Choose one name from your map. Put the send date on your calendar. A message you never send helps nobody.

    Keep adults in the loop

    Send from your own email or your parent’s, or hand your message to a parent to pass on. Ask a parent or youth leader to read it first. This keeps you safe and makes the adult more comfortable saying yes. More on the Rules page.

    Hear nothing?

    Wait one week. Send one short follow-up. Then move on to the next name. Most “no reply” means a busy person, not a no.

    Template: a parent’s coworker

    Hi Mr. Chen, this is Maya. I'm a sophomore at Davis High, and my mom, Priya Shah, works with you. I'm curious about careers in civil engineering. Could I ask you a few questions for 15 minutes, by phone or in person, sometime this month? Totally fine if you're busy. Thank you for reading this!

    Template: a local business owner

    Hi Ms. Alvarez, my name is Jordan and I'm a junior at Oakland Tech. I come into your bakery most Saturdays with my brother. I'd love to learn how a small food business works. Could I help out on a weekend for a few hours, or ask you a few questions for 15 minutes? No pressure if the timing is not right. Thank you!

    Template: a teacher, coach or counselor

    Hi Coach Nguyen, this is Sam from third period. I'm trying to find a volunteer or internship opportunity for next summer, and I'm interested in sports medicine. Do you know anyone I could talk to for 15 minutes, or a program you would recommend? Thank you for your time!

    Template: someone from church

    Hi Mr. Park, this is Daniel from the high school group. My mom told me you work in software. I'm in 11th grade and curious about coding as a career. Could I ask you a few questions for 15 minutes after service sometime? Totally fine if you're busy. Thank you!

    Template: the one-week follow-up

    Hi Mrs. Lee, I'm following up on my note from last week. I know you are busy. If a 15-minute chat does not work, could you point me to someone else who might be open to one? Thank you again!

    Template: the thank-you note

    Hi Mr. Chen, thank you for talking with me today. I did not know that engineers write so much for permits, and I'm going to look up the class you mentioned. I'll let you know how it goes. Thanks again for your time!

    These are examples with made-up names. Swap in your own details and your own words.

    4. After the yes

    Before

    Prepare two questions

    • How did you get into this work?
    • What does a normal day look like?
    • What is the hardest part of your job?
    • What would you do differently if you were my age?
    • Who else should I talk to?
    During

    Show up well

    • Arrive five minutes early.
    • Put your phone away.
    • Bring a notebook and write things down.
    • Dress one step nicer than the people there.
    • Stop at the time you promised, unless they keep going.
    After

    Keep the door open

    • Send a thank-you within 24 hours.
    • Do the thing you said you would do, then tell them.
    • Ask who else you should talk to.
    • Check in every few months with one line about what you did.

    Say yes to small stuff

    Filing, setup, cleanup, running errands. Small jobs done well are how people decide to give you bigger ones.

    Walking into a local business

    Go with a parent the first time, during a slow hour (mid-morning on a weekday works for many shops). Say: “Hi, I'm Jordan, a junior at Oakland Tech. I'd love to learn how your business works. Is there a good time to ask the owner a few questions for 15 minutes?” Leave a short note with your name, grade and a way to reach a parent.

    Skip the walk-in if the place is busy, if you are alone and not comfortable, or if it is a business like a bank or a clinic that does not talk to walk-ins. Use the message instead.
