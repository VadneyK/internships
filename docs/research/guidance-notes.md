# 1. CALIFORNIA YOUTH WORK RULES

## 1.1 Who needs a work permit

- Rule: With limited exceptions, every minor under 18 who is employed in California must have a Permit to Employ and Work. It is required even when school is not in session, and it is issued for a specific job at a specific address. [F] DLSE "California Child Labor Laws" (2013 digest) ch. 3, citing EC 49160 and LC 1299: https://www.dir.ca.gov/dlse/ChildLaborLawPamphlet.pdf ; same points in DIR employer sheet https://www.dir.ca.gov/dlse/Complying-with-Child-Labor-Laws-English.pdf [F]
- Not needed if the minor is a high school graduate or holds a certificate of proficiency (a certificate of proficiency generally requires age 16, or a year of 10th grade). [F] same pamphlet, ch. 3 "Exclusions" (EC 49101, LC 1286)
- Age floor for a permit: any minor at least 12 may be issued one, but few jobs are open to under 14. [F] same pamphlet (EC 49111)
- Forms: "Statement of Intent to Employ a Minor and Request for a Work Permit - Certificate of Age" (CDE Form B1-1) and "Permit to Employ and Work" (CDE Form B1-4). The B1-4 itself says a permit shall not be issued until the B1-1 has been signed by the parent or guardian (or foster parent, caregiver, or residential shelter provider) and filed with the issuing authority (EC 49110(c)). [F] https://www.dir.ca.gov/dlse/dlseformB1-4.pdf (read raw text)
- The state permit also serves as the federal certificate of age. [F] pamphlet ch. 3

## 1.2 Step by step: who signs what, how long it takes

1. Get a real job offer first. The minor gets the B1-1 from the school after "obtaining a promise of employment." [S] CDE FAQ text as shown on a district page https://shs.mpusd.net/work-permit ; consistent with [F] DLSE pamphlet ch. 3 ("after an employer agrees to hire a minor"). Palo Alto USD states it does not issue blank permits: students must have an offer. [F] https://gunn.pausd.org/fs/pages/3125 (district policy, not state law)
2. Minor fills in the minor section; the employer fills in the employer section; the parent or guardian signs; the prospective supervisor/employer signs. [F] DLSE pamphlet ch. 3 (EC 49162, 49163) and DIR sheet ("signed by the minor's parent or guardian and the employer").
3. Return the completed B1-1 to the school (the school the minor attends, or for charter schools either the school or the chartering district). [F] pamphlet ch. 3. The school's authorized issuer verifies the information and may issue the B1-4. [S] CDE FAQ via https://shs.mpusd.net/work-permit
4. The issuer fills in the maximum hours per school day, non-school day, and week (school in session and out), the range of hours, and any limits; the minor signs the permit; the issuer certifies and signs. [F] B1-4 form text.
5. The employer must keep the permit on file and available for inspection by school and labor officials. [F] DIR employer sheet.
6. Renewal: permits issued during the school year expire five days after the opening of the next school year and must be renewed. [F] B1-4 form (EC 49118) and DIR sheet.
7. A school may impose extra rules (for example a 2.0 GPA minimum) and may restrict hours below the legal maximum; no law forces a school to issue the maximum hours. [S] CDE FAQ via mpusd page; [F] pamphlet ch. 3 ("school officials may impose additional restrictions")
8. How long: it varies by district. Examples: Monterey Peninsula USD high school says a minimum of 24 hours (1 business day) [F] https://shs.mpusd.net/work-permit ; Palo Alto USD says expect 4 to 5 days and processes year round except holidays and school breaks [F] https://gunn.pausd.org/fs/pages/3125 ; a search-result roundup said districts range from 24 hours to about 5 business days [S]. Tell teens to start 1 to 2 weeks before the first shift. [SYN]
9. SSN: the B1-1 and B1-4 ask for the minor's Social Security number. The CDE FAQ says the statutes require the number, not that the card itself be presented. [S] mpusd mirror of the CDE FAQ; the B1-4 has an SSN field [F]. A teen without an SSN should ask the school counselor how the district handles it. [TODO verify with CDE or a district]
10. Home-schooled, private, and charter students: the school of enrollment (or the charter authority) issues it. [F] pamphlet (charter); PAUSD page says private/charter/home-schooled students go to their school site administrator [F] https://gunn.pausd.org/fs/pages/3125
11. Work for an employment agency: the end site where the minor physically works is listed as the employer (PAUSD policy). [F] same page

## 1.3 Who is exempt from the permit

All [F] from the DIR "Minors Summary Charts" (current DIR chart) https://www.dir.ca.gov/dlse/MinorsSummaryCharts.pdf (read raw text) and the 2013 pamphlet ch. 3:

- Self-employed minors (for example a teen running their own lawn or tutoring business).
- Irregular odd jobs in private homes: babysitting, lawn mowing, leaf raking, yard work. (Legal basis cited in the pamphlet is a 1951 Attorney General opinion.)
- Newspaper or magazine carriers who are self-employed on a regular route; the chart says newscarriers must be at least 12. The pamphlet says minors at least 14 who deliver newspapers need no permit whether or not self-employed.
- A parent or guardian employing their own minor child in agriculture, horticulture, viticulture or domestic labor on property the parent owns, operates or controls: no permit (LC 1394).
- Family business rule: parents who employ their kids in a commercial enterprise (mercantile, manufacturing, and so on) DO need the standard permit, and the usual hour and job limits apply. Domestic and agricultural work on the parent's property is the exception. [F] pamphlet ch. 3 (EC 49141) and chart note. Federal law adds that parents may employ their own children under 16 in non-hazardous, non-mining, non-manufacturing work. [F] chart
- Unpaid volunteers and unpaid trainees in an in-school placement with written parental permission on file with the school (5 CCR 10121). [F] https://law.cornell.edu/regulations/california/5-CCR-10121 (read raw text). See 1.12 for B1-6.
- State and local agencies that directly employ minors are exempt from the Labor Code child-labor permit rules (but not from federal FLSA rules). [F] pamphlet ch. 3
- Entertainment is the opposite case: it needs DLSE-issued permits (employer's "Permit to Employ Minors in the Entertainment Industry" and the minor's "Entertainment Work Permit"), not the school permit. [F] pamphlet ch. 3 and ch. 9; chart.
- Practical caution for the guide: private-residence work that is regular, or through an agency, is not "irregular odd jobs." Some districts refuse permits for work inside private homes (PAUSD policy [F]).

## 1.4 Ages 12 and 13

All [F] DIR chart (https://www.dir.ca.gov/dlse/MinorsSummaryCharts.pdf) and pamphlet ch. 5 and 6:

- A permit is required. They may be employed ONLY on non-school days (school holidays and vacations, usually construed to include weekends). Never before, during or after school on a school day (EC 49111).
- Max 8 hours a day and 40 hours a week; spread of hours 7 a.m. to 7 p.m., extended to 9 p.m. from June 1 through Labor Day (LC 1391).
- School-year daily and weekly hour limits are not set in statute; local school officials set them on the permit. The pamphlet says it is very unlikely a school would allow more than the 3 per day and 18 per week that 14 and 15 year olds get.
- Allowed work: personal attendants (includes babysitting and companionship), household occupations, newspaper carrying, and entertainment (with DLSE permits). Not allowed: jobs restricted to 14 and up, any occupation hazardous for under 16 or under 18, and Work Experience Education programs.
- Federal law (FLSA): generally bars employing children under 14 in non-agricultural work except exempt jobs such as newspaper delivery and acting. [F] DOL Fact Sheet 43 (fetch summary) https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/fact-sheets/43-child-labor-non-agriculture ; the DIR chart lists the same federal exemptions. Where state and federal law differ the more protective standard applies. [F] DIR chart header
- Bottom line for the guide: 12 and 13 year olds can legally do paid yard work, babysitting, pet care and family-adjacent odd jobs, and paid newspaper carrying. A regular company job is essentially not available. [SYN, consistent with above]

## 1.5 Ages 14 and 15 (hours, windows)

All [F] DIR chart and pamphlet ch. 5; DIR employer sheet (https://www.dir.ca.gov/dlse/Complying-with-Child-Labor-Laws-English.pdf) confirms the table of key limitations:

- School in session: 3 hours on a school day (all outside school hours), 8 hours on a non-school day, 18 hours per week. Must have completed 7th grade to work while school is in session (EC 49112).
- School not in session: 8 hours a day, 40 hours a week.
- Time-of-day window: 7 a.m. to 7 p.m. all year, extended to 9 p.m. from June 1 through Labor Day. (Applies in both the school year and summer; there is no separate school-year window.)
- "School day" means a day the minor is required to attend 240 minutes or more. [F] chart footnote (LC 1391(b)); "school in session" follows the county public school calendar. [F] chart footnote
- Work Experience Education (WEE) enrollees may work up to 23 hours a week, some during school hours. [F] chart
- Federal rules for 14 and 15 are the same on hours (3 per school day, 8 non-school day, 18 per week, 7 a.m. to 7 p.m., 9 p.m. June 1 to Labor Day). [F] DOL Fact Sheet 43 (fetch summary)
- Allowed job types: office and clerical, cashiering, selling, bagging, stocking, errands by foot/bike/transit, clean-up (no power mowers), kitchen work with limited machines, cooking only in plain view of customers and not as sole duty. Details in 1.8. [F] chart

## 1.6 Ages 16 and 17 (hours, windows)

All [F] DIR chart and pamphlet ch. 5:

- School in session: 4 hours on a school day; 8 hours on a non-school day or on any day before a non-school day; 48 hours per week.
- School not in session: 8 hours a day, 48 per week.
- Window: 5 a.m. to 10 p.m.; until 12:30 a.m. on an evening before a non-school day. WEE students may work until 12:30 a.m. with approval and must then be paid at least adult minimum wage for those hours. Messengers 6 a.m. to 9 p.m.
- Overtime applies (state and federal). Part-time students at continuation schools have separate rules.
- Same permit requirement applies. Must have completed 7th grade to work while school is in session (EC 49112), same as for 14 and 15 year olds. [F] DIR chart

## 1.7 Breaks and related worker rights

- Meal: if you work more than 5 hours in a day you get a 30-minute uninterrupted unpaid meal break; if the shift is no more than 6 hours total you can waive it. Rest: a paid 10-minute rest break for every 4 hours worked. Paid sick leave: 1 hour earned per 30 hours worked after 30 days with the employer; employer may cap use at 5 days/40 hours per year. Retaliation for using these rights is illegal. [F] Labor Commissioner Youth Advocate brochure https://dir.ca.gov/dlse/Youth-Advocate-Brochure.pdf (read raw text; note its wage table is outdated, see 1.9)
- One day of rest in seven, with few exceptions. [F] DIR chart (LC 551 to 554)
- Employers cannot take tips or make illegal deductions. [F] DIR employer sheet (LC 1196)
- Employers with five or more employees that employ minors must train supervisors of minors as mandated reporters of child abuse (AB 1963, 2020). [F] DIR employer sheet

## 1.8 Prohibited and restricted occupations

[F] DIR chart (partial lists; the chart says "see text") https://www.dir.ca.gov/dlse/MinorsSummaryCharts.pdf :

- All minors under 18: federally declared hazardous jobs, including manufacturing/storing explosives, motor vehicle driving and outside helper, logging and sawmilling, power-driven woodworking machines and saws, power hoisting (including forklifts), roofing, excavation, wrecking and demolition, power metal-forming and bakery and paper-products machines, meat slaughtering/packing/processing, mining, brick/tile manufacturing, exposure to radioactive substances.
- Under 18, state law only: certain gas-station work (pits, racks, lifts, tire inflation on rim with retainer ring), any activity in the part of an establishment primarily designed for on-site alcohol consumption, selling alcohol for off-site consumption or lottery tickets unless constantly supervised by someone 21 or older.
- Under 16 adds: manufacturing/processing, power-driven machinery, driving or helping on motor vehicles, public messenger service, construction (including demolition and repair), warehousing and storage (except clerical), maintenance/repair of equipment, work on ladders/scaffolds, operating food slicers/grinders/mixers, freezers/meat coolers, loading/unloading trucks, commercial cooking outside plain view, door-to-door sales unless strict supervision rules are met, and being on boats or near aircraft/blades. Gas pumping and car washing are federally allowed at 14 and 15 but California requires age 16.
- Door-to-door sales for under 16 (for example cookie or candy fundraising for pay) only in pairs, one adult supervisor per 10 minors, within sight or sound every 15 minutes, home daily, within 50 miles, and employer/supervisor registered with DLSE if over 10 miles from home. [F] chart
- Agriculture: under 16 cannot operate tractors over 20 PTO horsepower, handle certain pesticides, or work from heights over 20 feet, among other limits. [F] chart
- Training in hazardous jobs is allowed for 16 and 17 only through bona fide training programs. [F] pamphlet ch. 8 / chart

## 1.10 Do interns have to be paid? (California and federal)

- Federal (DOL Fact Sheet 71, updated January 2018): the "primary beneficiary test." Seven non-exclusive factors weighing whether the intern or the employer benefits more: (1) whether both sides clearly understand there is no expectation of pay (any promise of pay suggests employee); (2) training similar to an educational environment; (3) tie to formal education such as coursework or credit; (4) timing that fits the academic calendar; (5) limited duration that matches the learning; (6) work that complements, rather than displaces, paid staff; (7) understanding that there is no entitlement to a job afterward. If the intern is an employee, minimum wage and overtime apply. [F] (fetch summary) https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/fact-sheets/71-flsa-internships . The test applies to for-profit employers; interns at public agencies and nonprofit charities may be volunteers. [K, not shown on the fetch summary]
- Federal bulletin FAB 2018-2 adopted this test. [S] https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/field-assistance-bulletins/2018-2 (appeared in search; not opened). I did not find any later change.
- California: In April 2010 the DLSE issued an opinion letter saying it would follow the federal approach to unpaid interns, replacing its older eleven-factor test with the DOL's then six-factor test. [S] law-firm summaries (Littler, WSGR) found in search; I did not open the DLSE letter. California has not, to my knowledge, formally adopted the 2018 seven-factor wording. [K] Practical rule for the guide: an unpaid internship at a for-profit company in California is legal only if the intern, not the company, is the primary beneficiary (training-focused, school-linked, no replacing staff); otherwise it must be a paid job with minimum wage, and minors also need a work permit. A teen should be wary of any for-profit "unpaid internship" that looks like ordinary work.
- Berkeley Career Engagement says unpaid roles must follow FLSA/legal guidelines and that employers may require academic credit to satisfy state and federal law. [F] https://career.berkeley.edu/find-opportunities/internships/
- Teen-specific: a paid internship for a minor is a job: it needs the work permit, hour limits and minimum wage. [SYN from 1.1 to 1.9]

## 1.11 Parent's company

Allowed. A parent who employs their minor child in a business (mercantile, manufacturing or similar) must get the standard permit and follows the same age, hour and job limits as any employer; the permit exception is only for domestic, agricultural, horticultural or viticultural work on property the parent owns, operates or controls. [F] DLSE 2013 pamphlet ch. 3 and ch. 10 (EC 49141, LC 1394) and the DIR chart. The DIR chart also says parents are exempt from the wage-payment requirements for some of those home-property jobs, so the safest guide wording is: "treat it like a real job: permit, hours, and pay." For tax, special payroll-tax rules can apply to a child employed by a parent's sole proprietorship. [K, not verified; TODO]

## 1.12 B1-6 school-arranged work experience (unpaid)

- CDE Form B1-6 is "Request for Volunteer/Unpaid Trainee Authorization for Minor." It is for school-supported volunteering and unpaid internships (placements the school arranges), not for volunteering the student arranged alone. The form has the minor and school info, the host business/agency and a description of services, and parent permission. Under Education Code 51769 the school/LEA is generally responsible for workers' compensation insurance during the unpaid placement. [S] search-result summary of the OCDE-hosted PDF (the PDF link returned 404 when I tried it; CDE site was bot-blocked)
- The legal basis that no work permit is needed for unpaid in-school trainees and volunteers: 5 CCR 10121 requires (a) no pay or financial reimbursement, (b) no employer-employee relationship under the FLSA and the minor is an unpaid trainee, volunteer or in-school placement, and (c) parental permission with written evidence filed with the school. [F] https://law.cornell.edu/regulations/california/5-CCR-10121 (read raw text)
- Work Experience Education (WEE, paid or unpaid school-supervised work) is a different school program: students must be at least 16 to enroll (with exceptions); 14 and 15 year olds in WEE may work up to 23 hours a week. [F] pamphlet ch. 3 and DIR chart

## 1.13 Volunteering for minors at nonprofits and hospitals

- DLSE: a volunteer is generally someone who works for public service, religious or humanitarian reasons without promise, expectation or receipt of pay. Classification depends on the parties' intent. [S] CalChamber/HR Watchdog summaries https://hrwatchdog.calchamber.com/2025/12/who-is-a-volunteer-best-practices-for-adult-minor-volunteers/ (search result; not opened)
- No state work permit is needed for a true volunteer if 5 CCR 10121's conditions are met (written parental permission on file with the school, no pay). [F] 5 CCR 10121 above. Practice varies by host: many hospitals and nonprofits set their own minimum age, apply forms and required orientation. [K; TODO get one Northern California hospital teen volunteer page]
- Organizations that serve minors often require Live Scan fingerprinting, mandated-reporter training and child-abuse-prevention policies for adult volunteers. [S] CalChamber summary. Example: the Diocese of Oakland requires abuse-prevention training before volunteering and every three years, and Live Scan clearance for volunteers with direct, ongoing contact with minors. [F] https://www.oakdiocese.org/safe-environment (read raw text)
- Labor Code 1294.1 bars minors under 16 from construction work, paid or unpaid. [S] CalChamber summary
- Federal: volunteering for a nonprofit or public agency is fine if it is truly voluntary and not for a for-profit business. [K; the DOL fact sheet I tried to open (#14A) was blocked]

## 1.14 Cash, 1099, or W-2 for a teen job

- An employee gets a W-2. A teen paid for regular work by a business is almost always an employee: the employer must withhold payroll taxes, keep time and pay records, and carry workers' compensation for minors it employs. [F] DIR employer sheet (recordkeeping and minimum wage points), [K] for tax withholding specifics.
- A true independent contractor (1099) must be running their own business: set own hours and methods, serve more than one customer, supply own tools. California applies the "ABC test" (Labor Code 2775) to decide employee versus contractor; most hourly teen jobs would not pass it. [K, not opened this session; tag as [S] for the guide until verified]. A search result claimed it is "illegal to hire anyone under 18 as an independent contractor"; I could NOT confirm that and DIR's chart expressly recognizes "self-employed minors," so do not print that claim. [S, unverified]
- Self-employed teens (babysitting, lawn mowing, tutoring) are not employees: no permit needed [F], but net self-employment earnings of $400 or more require filing and self-employment tax (Schedule SE). [F] IRS https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/tax-tips-from-the-irs-for-students-starting-a-summer-job (fetch summary)
- Being paid in cash does not change the legal status; cash wages are still taxable and an employer still owes the minimum wage. [K/SYN] A teen who is paid "under the table" has weaker proof of earnings, and has no pay stubs to support a wage claim. [SYN]

## 1.15 Tax basics for a teen's first job

- W-4: every new employee completes a Form W-4 for each employer so the employer knows how much federal income tax to withhold; with more than one job, check total withholding with the IRS withholding estimator. Tips are taxable. [F] IRS tips page above (fetch summary)
- Some teens expect no tax liability and can claim "exempt" on the W-4 to stop federal income tax withholding, but Social Security and Medicare (FICA) are still withheld. [S] general tax-explainer snippets; verify on irs.gov before printing.
- Do teens file? A teen who can be claimed as a dependent files a return if, for tax year 2025, single dependent under 65 and not blind: earned income over $15,750, or unearned income over $1,350, or gross income over the larger of $1,350 or (earned income up to $15,300 plus $450). [F] IRS Pub 501 (2025), Table 2, read raw text: https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p501.pdf
- For tax year 2026 the standard deduction for a single filer rises to $16,100. [F] IRS newsroom https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/irs-releases-tax-inflation-adjustments-for-tax-year-2026-including-amendments-from-the-one-big-beautiful-bill (fetch summary). A secondary source says dependents with only earned income file if over $16,100 and use "greater of $1,350 or earned income plus $450" as the dependent standard deduction. [S] ustax.tools / ptmoney snippets; not verified on irs.gov.
- Even if no filing is required, a teen who had tax withheld should file to get the withholding refunded. [K]
- California has its own dependent filing rules based on gross income versus a standard deduction. [S] FTB/TaxSlayer snippets; verify at ftb.ca.gov. [TODO]
- Social Security number: needed for payroll and for the work permit forms (number, not the card, per CDE FAQ mirror [S]). Employers use Form I-9 to verify identity and work authorization for every new hire; a teen applying for a new SSN may start and provide it later. [S] I-9 explainer snippets; USCIS I-9 Central confirms every employer must complete I-9 for each hire [F] https://www.uscis.gov/i-9-central . For minors under 18 without ID, special I-9 rules exist (school record, report card, etc.). [K; TODO verify against USCIS M-274]

## 1.16 Reporting wage theft

- Contact the Labor Commissioner's Office (DLSE). Helpline 833-LCO-INFO (833-526-4636), 8 a.m. to 5 p.m. Monday to Friday, English and Spanish; wagetheftisacrime.com. [F] DIR release 2026-66
- File a wage claim online, by email, mail, or in person at a district office. Gather employer name and address, a record of hours worked, all paystubs, and names of supervisors. After filing, a settlement conference is typically scheduled, then a hearing if unresolved. Filing windows: 1 year for bounced-check penalties or denied access to payroll records; 2 years for oral promises above minimum wage; 3 years for minimum wage, overtime, unpaid breaks, sick leave, illegal deductions; 4 years for written contracts. [F] (fetch summary) https://www.dir.ca.gov/dlse/howtofilewageclaim.htm

## 2.2 Safe-practice rules for minors contacting adults online

## 3.2 Common red flags (all [F] unless noted; FTC job scam page https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/job-scams and fake-check page https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/how-spot-avoid-and-report-fake-check-scams )

- Pay to apply or pay for "training," "starter kits," "certifications," or "placement." Honest employers, including the federal government, never charge to hire you. Honest placement firms typically do not charge candidates; the hiring company pays them.
- Check-cashing: a "boss" sends a check, tells you to deposit it and send part back, buy gift cards, or buy crypto. The check is fake; the bank can take the money back weeks later.
- Reshipping: "your job" is receiving and forwarding packages.
- "Guaranteed" or easy high pay for little work, or working from home with no specifics.
- Task scams: an unexpected text or WhatsApp about online "tasks" (liking videos, "product boosting"), small early payouts, then a demand to deposit money to unlock earnings. [F] FTC data spotlight
- Unsolicited offers by text, WhatsApp, Telegram or email; interview only by chat app. [F for unexpected texts/WhatsApp (FTC); Telegram is [SYN] in the same pattern]
- Requests for SSN, bank account, or ID photos before you are actually hired and have a signed offer. [S/SYN; FTC job-scam page covers fees and fake checks, and an employer legitimately needs SSN and bank details only at onboarding. Verify with FTC "Employer background checks" guidance.]
- Vague company details, free-email addresses for a supposed company, no real website or address. [SYN consistent with FTC "search the name plus scam, review, complaint"]
- Government and postal jobs: always free to apply; use USAJobs.gov and usps.com/employment. [F]

## 3.3 Short checklist for teens (adapted from FTC advice) [F for each item from the FTC page; wording is SYN]

1. Did a stranger contact me first? Slow down.
2. Does it cost me anything (fee, kit, "refundable" deposit)? If yes, stop.
3. Did they send a check or ask me to move money? If yes, stop.
4. Search "[name] scam / review / complaint."
5. Show it to a parent or counselor before you reply. (The FTC suggests talking it over with someone you trust.)
6. For minors, also check: can this legally employ a minor my age, and will the school permit process work? A real employer will know about work permits. [SYN from Section 1]
7. If you lost money: contact your bank or payment app right away; report to ReportFraud.ftc.gov and the California Attorney General. [F] FTC job-scam page

## 4.2 Weak ties (Rajkumar, Saint-Jacques, Bojinov, Brynjolfsson, Aral, Science, 2022)

- Randomized experiments on LinkedIn's People You May Know algorithm varied weak ties in the networks of over 20 million people over 5 years, during which 2 billion new ties and 600,000 new jobs were created. Finding: the relationship is an inverted U; moderately weak ties (by mutual connections) and the weakest ties (by interaction intensity) created the most job mobility; in more digital industries weak ties helped more, in less digital industries strong ties helped more. [F] Science 377(6612):1304-1310, PMID 36107999, abstract read via PubMed API: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36107999/ (DOI 10.1126/science.abl4476)
- Caveats [SYN]: this is about adult LinkedIn members and job mobility, not high school students; it supports "talk to people one step outside your closest circle, such as a parent's colleague or a friend's neighbor." Do not claim it proves teens get internships this way.

## 4.3 Informational interview best practice (university career centers)

## 5.2 Distilled rules (consistent across sources)

- Length: one page. (Berkeley, MIT, UCSC all say one page; MIT: "stick to one page unless you have extensive experience.") For a teen: always one page.
- Format: simple, consistent, standard font 10 to 12 point, margins at least 0.5 inch (Berkeley: margins .5 to 1 inch; MIT: no smaller than 10 pt and at least half-inch margins; UCSC: 10 to 12 pt, margins no smaller than 0.5 inch). Use bold and italics sparingly. Use black text; color only sparingly.
- File format: Word or Google Doc exported to PDF; ATS-friendly means standard fonts and plain section titles, and avoid headers, footers, text boxes, tables, colors, pictures or graphics. (Berkeley ATS FAQ; UCSC; Yale templates). Name the file "FirstName_LastName_Resume.pdf." [SYN for file name]
- Header: name biggest; city and state (not full street address); phone; professional email; optional LinkedIn URL (16+ only). (UCSC says location is "City, State.") Leave off photos (MIT: photos generally not preferred for U.S. resumes), age, religion, health, marital status, and SSN. [F] MIT for photo and personal info; the SSN and full-address omission is [SYN] (UCSC shows city and state only).
- Section order for a teen with no jobs: Header, Education, Experience (broad meaning), Projects/Activities/Leadership, Skills, Awards (optional), Interests (optional). Berkeley: contact info, education and skills, plus any work, research, volunteer, project experience; UCSC: Header, Education, Experience (employment, internships, projects, athletics, community organizations, volunteering), Skills (optional).
- What counts as experience: MIT: "experience comes in many forms," including class projects, competitions, personal projects, leadership roles; UCSC: employment, internships, projects (academic or personal), research, athletics, community organizations, volunteering; Berkeley: volunteer work counts, "it is not important that you were paid." For a teen: babysitting, yard work, tutoring, helping at a family business, sports team captain, club officer, church or school volunteering, Scout projects, a website or app you built, a YouTube channel you ran.
- Bullets: start with a strong action verb; no first person; use past tense for finished work; add numbers (MIT: "if you gave a presentation, say how many attended; if you raised money, say how much"); show results not just duties; describe how you did it. UCSC: 1 to 5 bullets per experience; order each section most recent first.
- Tailor to the opportunity: pick the experiences that show the skills in the posting. (Berkeley, MIT)
- Interests/hobbies: optional and first to cut; be specific, not a list. (Berkeley)
- References: do not list them on the resume; have a separate list ready and give it if asked. "References available upon request" is unnecessary. (MIT; UCSC says do not include references on a resume)
- GPA: Berkeley says include if 3.0 or above ("if you've got it, flaunt it"); UCSC includes GPA if above 3.0; MIT says write "GPA: N/A" for first-year students without one. For a teen: put GPA only if it helps (3.0 or higher unweighted is the college-center rule of thumb; none of these sources addresses high school specifically). [F for the college rule; teen application is SYN]
- Proofread and have two people read it. (MIT, Berkeley)
- Email address: professional name-based address (firstname.lastname). Not sourced from the pages I read. [SYN] Do not use the school email if the school may deactivate it after graduation. [SYN]
- Contact: A phone number a parent also answers may be good for under-16s. [SYN]

## 5.3 "Brag list" approach

- Source for the principle: Berkeley and MIT both say to start by generating a list of accomplishments, then pick the ones that match the job. Berkeley: "Generate a list of accomplishments to include in each section." MIT: keep a "master resume" detailing all experiences and awards, then build a targeted one-page version. [F]
- Brag-list prompts for a teen [SYN]: What did I make, fix, organize, win, lead, teach, save, sell, earn, or finish? What did people thank me for? What did I do every week for more than 3 months? What numbers go with it (people, hours, dollars, items, events, followers, pages, minutes)? What problem did I solve? What did I learn?
- Turn each item into a bullet: verb + what + number or result.

# 6. EMAIL AND DM TEMPLATES (all [SYN], built on Berkeley "5 Tips for Reaching Out", UCSC and UChicago networking pages; each body is under 90 words)

Rules that apply to all: keep it short; say how you know them first; ask for something small; copy a parent for adults you do not know (Section 2); proofread; use a normal subject line.

## 6.1 Ask a parent's coworker for a 15 minute chat

Subject: Quick question from [Parent's first name]'s daughter/son

Hi Ms. Chen,
I'm Alex Rivera, a 10th grader at Oakland Example High. My mom, Dana Rivera, said you work in civil engineering at the city. I'm curious about the field. Would you have 15 minutes in the next few weeks for a call or a short chat, so I can ask how you got started? I can work around your schedule. My mom is copied.
Thank you,
Alex

Why this works: it names the connection first, asks for information not a job, keeps the ask small, and gives a parent visibility. (Berkeley: state your connection first, do not ask for a job.)

## 6.2 Ask to shadow

Subject: Request to shadow for a morning, [your school] student

Hello Dr. Patel,
I'm Jordan Lee, a 9th grader at Example High School. My neighbor, Mr. Ortiz, said you run the vet clinic on Elm Street. I'm thinking about animal care as a career. Could I shadow you or a technician for a few hours on a school holiday, or watch for a morning? My parent can sign any form you need. I know you're busy, so even a short tour would help.
Thank you,
Jordan

Why this works: a time-boxed, low-burden request ("a few hours," "a tour"), offers paperwork help, and names who referred you. Note: some workplaces will say no for insurance or privacy reasons; that is not personal. (Hospitals and clinics may have privacy rules; the university job-shadow programs run 1 to 5 days. [F] UChicago page) 

## 6.3 Ask a local business owner

Subject: Weekend help at Maria's Bakery?

Hi Maria,
I'm Sam Nguyen, 15, and I live on Oak Street. I come into your bakery most Saturdays. I'm looking for a first job and would love to help with packaging orders, cleaning, or the front counter. I can work weekends and school holidays, and I know I need a work permit through my school. Could I send you my one-page resume, or stop by at a quiet time?
Thanks,
Sam

Why this works: it shows you are a real customer, names the hours you can work, and mentions the permit so the owner knows you understand the rules (Section 1: 14 and 15 year olds need a permit and have hour limits).

## 6.4 Follow up after no reply

Subject: Re: Quick question from [Parent's first name]'s daughter/son

Hi Ms. Chen,
I'm following up on my note from last week about a 15 minute chat. I know inboxes get busy. If it's easier, I can send two questions by email instead. If now isn't a good time, no problem at all.
Thank you,
Alex

Why this works: one polite follow-up after 5 to 7 days is accepted practice, and it offers a smaller option. Then stop. (UChicago: wait 5 to 7 days, send one nudge, then move on.)

## 6.5 Thank-you after help

Subject: Thank you, [Name]

Hi Ms. Chen,
Thank you for talking with me on Tuesday. I did not know city engineers also work on storm drains, and I'm going to look into the Engineering Club at school like you suggested. If it's okay, I'll send you an update at the end of the semester.
Thanks again,
Alex

Why this works: it is specific, shows you acted on advice, and sets up a reason for a later update. (Berkeley: thank-you within 1 to 2 days; UCSC: within 24 hours; keep in touch.)

## 6.6 Phone or in-person script for a small business

Walk-in script (about 20 seconds):
"Hi, I'm Sam. I live nearby and I'm looking for my first job. I can work weekends and school holidays. Is the owner or manager available for a minute, or is there a better time to come back? I brought a one-page resume."
If told no: "Thanks for your time. Is it okay if I check back in the spring?"
If told yes: get the manager's name and email; ask how they handle hours for under-18s; say you need a work permit through school.

When it is a bad idea [SYN]: during a rush (lunch, Saturday afternoon); at a business that is not allowed to hire your age (bars, certain restaurants, heavy equipment shops; see Section 1.8); at someone's home or a business where you would be alone with an adult in a closed space; when you are under 14 and the job is not an allowed one; or when you have not told a parent where you are going. Go with a parent or friend for the first one if you are nervous. Prefer a phone call or an email first for workplaces with machinery.

# 7. INTERVIEW AND FIRST-DAY BASICS FOR TEENS (8 to 10 bullets)

1. Practice answers out loud before the interview; practice builds comfort. [F] UCSC Interview Prep https://careers.ucsc.edu/toolkit/interview-prep/ (also: plan 1 to 2 minutes per answer)
2. Prepare 1 or 2 questions to ask; always ask at least one. Examples from UCSC: What are the top priorities for the person in this role over the next three months? What training is offered? What is your timetable for deciding? Teen-friendly additions: Who would I report to? What hours would I work and how are shifts scheduled? How do you handle work permits for under-18 workers? [F for the UCSC examples; additions SYN]
3. Arrive 10 to 15 minutes early; know exactly where you are going; silence your phone; be polite to everyone you meet. [F] UCSC
4. Dress: ask the person who scheduled the interview what the dress code is. [F] UCSC. Safe default for an office or professional setting: clean, neat, modest, closed-toe shoes, no strong scents (UCSC advises avoiding distracting scents). For a food-service or retail interview, neat casual is fine; for a first office day, ask your supervisor beforehand. [SYN]
5. Use specific examples with a simple story structure (STAR: situation, task, action, result). [F] UCSC
6. You do not need to apologize for lack of experience; focus on the positives of your training and what you have done. [F] UCSC
7. Bring what you need: a copy of your resume, the work permit paperwork (B1-1 if not yet issued, or the permit), a notebook, a pen, ID. [SYN based on Section 1]
8. Send a thank-you note within 24 hours; get a card or name so you know who to thank. [F] UCSC; also Berkeley (1 to 2 days).
9. Handling nerves: preparation is the recommended tool (UCSC: "Interviews can be nerve-wracking so it's important to prepare thoroughly"); practice aloud with a family member or counselor; arrive early; pause and breathe before answering; it is fine to say "Can I take a moment to think about that?" [F for the first two; last two SYN]
10. First day basics [SYN; no primary source retrieved]: arrive 10 minutes early; bring ID and any permit/I-9 documents; write down names; ask what to do when the work is done; ask when your breaks are (if you work more than 5 hours you are owed a 30 minute meal break and a paid 10 minute rest break per 4 hours, [F] Section 1.7); tell a supervisor right away if something feels unsafe or you are asked to do work you are not allowed to do at your age (Section 1.8).

# 8. THE "HOW TO FIND ONE" PLAYBOOK FOR GRADES 7 TO 9 (UNDER 16)

Key framing: for most 12 to 15 year olds, a formal "internship" is rare; what counts is real, documented experience. Age rules come from Section 1 [F] unless marked.

1. Neighborhood odd jobs (babysitting, pet care, yard work, tutoring younger kids). Age: any age for irregular odd jobs in private homes; no permit [F]; self-employed teens owe self-employment tax only above $400 net [F]. Parent should know every client. For 12 to 13, this is the main legal paid option. [F]
2. Own micro-business (car-wash, baking, crafts, a tech-help service for seniors). Self-employed minors need no permit [F]; some products need permits (food: California cottage food rules [K, not verified]).
3. Volunteering with a nonprofit, library, food pantry, animal shelter, or place of worship. No work permit if unpaid with written parental permission on file with the school (5 CCR 10121 [F]); each organization sets its own minimum age and may require parent forms and training [K, F for Diocese of Oakland rules].
4. Job shadowing (a half day to a few days) arranged through a parent's or teacher's network. No state rule against it, but employers decide; many will want a parent's permission. If a school arranges it, ask about CDE Form B1-6 [S]. University job-shadow programs run 1 to 5 days [F UChicago, a college program, as a model].
5. Informational interviews (15 minutes) with a parent's friend. See 6.1 and Section 4. 
6. School and community projects: robotics, coding, a school garden, a podcast, a science fair project. Real output you can show on a resume. [SYN]
7. Clubs and student government, with a role (treasurer, captain, editor). [SYN, supported by resume sources in Section 5]
8. Camps, then junior counselor or counselor-in-training (CIT) programs. Age rules vary by program; many start in the early to mid teens [K]. Camp counselors can be paid a weekly salary based on 85 percent of minimum wage under LC 1182.4, and organized camps have FLSA exemptions [F] 2013 pamphlet ch. 4; [TODO] confirm still operative.
9. City youth employment and summer job programs (for example Oakland, San Francisco, San Jose, Alameda County programs for 14 and up). [K, TODO verify each program's eligibility and dates on the official city sites]
10. Paid work with a permit once 14 (retail, food service, office and clerical; hours capped at 3 per school day / 18 per week, 8 per day / 40 per week out of school; 7 a.m. to 7 p.m., 9 p.m. June 1 to Labor Day). [F] Section 1.5. Newspaper delivery needs no permit (12+ per the DIR chart). [F]

Extras: community college or university pre-college programs and library teen advisory boards (free or low cost) [K]. Work Experience Education (school credit, with a work permit) generally starts at 16. [F]

For under 14: federal law bars most non-agricultural employment of under-14s, with exemptions such as newspaper delivery and acting; babysitting and yard work are the main legal options. [F] DOL Fact Sheet 43 (fetch summary); DIR chart.

# 9. FAQ (15 questions, short answers)

1. Do I need experience? No. Employers and universities expect a first resume to be built from school, volunteering, projects, and odd jobs; MIT says experience "comes in many forms" including class projects and personal projects; UCSC says you do not have to apologize for a lack of experience. [F] Section 5 and 7 sources.
2. Is unpaid legal? Sometimes. A true volunteer for a nonprofit or school-arranged unpaid placement is fine. An unpaid role at a for-profit company is legal only if the intern is the primary beneficiary under the DOL test; otherwise it must be paid at least minimum wage. [F] DOL Fact Sheet 71 (fetch summary); California follows a similar approach per a 2010 DLSE letter [S]. See 1.10.
3. Do I need a work permit for volunteering? Not if you are unpaid, not in an employer-employee relationship, and have parent permission on file with your school (5 CCR 10121). [F] If the school arranges the placement it may use form B1-6. [S]
4. Can I intern at my parent's company? Yes, but parents who employ their kids in a business (as opposed to domestic or farm work on their own property) still need a work permit and must follow the age, hour and job rules; wages are required for a regular employee. [F] DIR chart and 2013 pamphlet; see 1.11.
5. What if I have a bad GPA? UC reviews GPA in the context of your opportunities and also considers special projects, leadership, significant community service, and marked recent improvement; it lists "need to work" among the circumstances it considers. [F] https://admission.universityofcalifornia.edu/how-to-apply/applying-as-a-freshman/how-applications-are-reviewed.html . For a work permit, some schools require a minimum GPA (for example 2.0) at their discretion. [F] Monterey Peninsula USD page https://shs.mpusd.net/work-permit ; check your school. A GPA below 3.0 is usually left off a resume (college career center rule of thumb [F] Berkeley, UCSC).
6. What if I'm shy? Use email, start with someone you already know, and use the templates; ask for 15 minutes with a parent's friend, not a stranger; practice your first sentence aloud. UChicago: non-responses are rarely about you. [F] UChicago page; templates SYN.
7. Do internships matter for college admissions? Not as a required item. UC says it looks beyond grades and may consider special projects, achievements, significant community service, leadership, and work. It does not say internships earn extra points; a paid internship brokered for a fee is not needed. [F] UC "How applications are reviewed" (see above) and the UC Personal Insight Questions, which ask about leadership, talents, educational opportunity, a challenge, community contributions and other topics https://admission.universityofcalifornia.edu/how-to-apply/applying-as-a-freshman/personal-insight-questions.html [F]. I did not find a specific UC or admissions-officer statement ranking internships. [TODO]
8. How many applications is normal? No teen-specific data found. For college seniors, Handshake reports the class of 2026 submitted 23 applications per full-time job, up from 20.8 and from 11 for the class of 2023. [F] https://joinhandshake.com/research/economic-research/labor-market-retro-january-2026/ UChicago advises a practical start of 3 to 5 outreach messages per week during an active search. [F] For a teen, apply to 5 to 10 well-matched local places rather than 100. [SYN]
9. International students, citizenship, work authorization? In the U.S., employers must verify identity and work authorization on Form I-9 for every hire, regardless of age. [F] https://www.uscis.gov/i-9-central . Whether an international student on an F-1 or other visa may work depends on the visa and may be restricted. [K, TODO confirm with USCIS/ school international office]. Unpaid volunteering is usually different from employment but check the visa terms. [K]
10. Immigration status and teen jobs (basics, careful): California labor law protects workers regardless of immigration status; the Labor Commissioner does not ask about immigration status and workers do not have to disclose it to file a wage or retaliation claim. [F] DIR release 2026-66. This does NOT mean a person without work authorization can lawfully be hired; employment eligibility rules are federal. Families with questions should talk to a qualified immigration attorney or a legal aid organization. [F/SYN]
11. How do I get references? Ask a teacher, coach, club advisor, volunteer supervisor, or someone you did a job for (a neighbor whose pet you cared for); ask them first, tell them what the job is, give them your resume, and thank them. Keep references on a separate sheet; do not put them on the resume unless asked. [F] MIT and UCSC on keeping references off the resume; ask-first practice [SYN].
12. Do I need LinkedIn? No. LinkedIn is for 16 and up. [F] UA 2.1. Email and a one-page resume are enough. 
13. What if I get no reply? One polite follow-up after 5 to 7 days, then move on; try someone else. [F] UChicago.
14. What is a fair wage and who do I tell if I'm underpaid? California minimum wage is $16.90 in 2026 and $17.40 starting Jan 1, 2027, or higher in some cities. [F] Section 1.9. Report underpayment to the Labor Commissioner at 833-526-4636. [F]
15. How do I know a job or internship is a scam? If you must pay, deposit a check, move money, or you were contacted first by text or WhatsApp about easy online "tasks," it is a scam. [F] FTC. See Section 3 checklist.
