Know your rights

Safe at work

Most jobs are fine. This page is for the day something is not: a task that feels unsafe, an injury, or someone who treats you badly. It shows what official pages say, and who to call. Researched in October 2026.

Tell a trusted adult, then use these numbers

    • US OSHA (workplace safety): 1-800-321-OSHA (6742) · TTY: 1-877-889-5627
    • US Department of Labor, Wage and Hour Division: 1-866-4-US-WAGE (1-866-487-9243)
    • US EEOC: 1-800-669-4000 · TTY: 1-800-669-6820 · ASL video phone: 1-844-234-5122

    More numbers, including California, Georgia, New York and Illinois, are in Who to call.

    Got an offer from someone you do not know?

    Stop and look for these red flags before you answer:

    • You were recruited by a message from a stranger, with no company name.
    • The pay is cash only, with no forms and no pay stub.
    • They ask for photos, an ID or your address before an interview.
    • They will not talk to a parent.
    • They ask you to pay or buy something first.

    Show a parent before you reply.

    This page covers federal rules plus California, Georgia, New York and Illinois. In another state the rules and the numbers may be different. See the work permit finder for your state, and the number to call.

    1. Your right to a safe workplace and training

    OSHA says young workers have the right to a safe place to work and to safety training they can understand. Employers must pay for most required safety gear. Ask questions if something seems unsafe. New York's youth page says a worker can say no to a task that risks serious injury or death. The California pages we read talk about safety, heat and training, but do not say in plain words that a teen may refuse unsafe work. The Georgia and Illinois youth pages we read do not list a right to safe work or training for teens, so this page does not claim one for those states.

    OSHA lists a safe workplace as a right of young workers.

    Work in a safe place.

    Source: US OSHA, Safe Work for Young Workers

    OSHA says workers should get safety training in a language they understand.

    Receive safety and health training in a language that you understand.

    Source: US OSHA, Safe Work for Young Workers

    OSHA says you can ask questions when something seems unsafe.

    Ask questions if you don't understand instructions or if something seems unsafe.

    Source: US OSHA, Safe Work for Young Workers

    OSHA says you should be trained on required safety gear.

    Use and be trained on required safety gear, such as hard hats, goggles and ear plugs.

    Source: US OSHA, Safe Work for Young Workers

    OSHA says the employer must tell you what to do if you get hurt.

    Tell you what to do if you get hurt on the job.

    Source: US OSHA, Safe Work for Young Workers

    OSHA says employers pay for most safety gear.

    Employers must also pay for most types of required safety gear.

    Source: US OSHA, Parents and Educators

    OSHA says you can file a confidential complaint about a serious hazard.

    File a confidential complaint with OSHA if you believe there is a serious hazard or that your employer is not following OSHA standards.

    Source: US OSHA, Safe Work for Young Workers

    OSHA tells young workers to report unsafe conditions to a supervisor.

    Report unsafe conditions to a shift/team leader or supervisor.

    Source: US OSHA, Safe Work for Young Workers

    For parents: OSHA says to help your teen report hazards, and to OSHA if needed.

    Help your young worker report hazards to managers or, if necessary, to OSHA if a work environment seems unsafe.

    Source: US OSHA, Parents and Educators

    New York lists a safe workplace in its Youth Workers Bill of Rights.

    You have the right to a safe workplace

    Source: New York State Department of Labor, Youth Worker Information Hub

    New York says a young worker may say no to a task that risks serious injury or death.

    If your employer asks you to do something that poses a risk of serious injury or death you have the right to say no.

    Source: New York State Department of Labor, Youth Worker Information Hub

    California's July 2026 release says young workers may be especially at risk from heat, and says heat rules include training.

    Young workers may be especially vulnerable

    Source: California Department of Industrial Relations, News Release 2026-59

    California says safety complaints can be filed confidentially (the page lists a Cal/OSHA phone number).

    Complaints about workplace safety and health hazards can be filed confidentially with

    Source: California Department of Industrial Relations, News Release 2026-59

    Georgia's child labor FAQ says the child labor rules exist to keep minors safe. It does not list a teen right to safety training.

    ensure the safety of minors in the work place.

    Source: Georgia Department of Labor, Individuals FAQs, Child Labor

    2. Jobs under 18 may not do

    Federal law sets 18 as the minimum age for jobs the Secretary of Labor calls hazardous. The US Department of Labor page lists these in plain words. Machines: meat slicers and similar machines, power woodworking machines, balers and trash compactors, forklifts and similar lifting equipment, bakery machines, metal-forming machines and power saws. Other work: roofing, driving as a job, explosives, demolition, mining, radioactive materials, brick and tile making, trenching deeper than four feet, and logging or sawmills. Some of these have narrow exceptions, so the page uses words like generally and most. Ages 14 and 15 have extra limits. Georgia, Illinois and New York have their own lists too.

    The 18-year minimum age for hazardous nonfarm jobs.

    The FLSA establishes an 18-year minimum age for those nonagricultural occupations

    Source: US DOL Wage and Hour Division, Fact Sheet 43 (revised December 2016)

    Ages 14 and 15 also cannot do the hazardous jobs.

    Child Labor Regulation No. 3 also bans 14- and 15-year-olds from performing any work proscribed by the HOs.

    Source: US DOL Wage and Hour Division, Fact Sheet 43

    The federal fact sheet lists 17 Hazardous Occupations Orders. One of them covers roofing.

    Roofing operations and work performed on or about a roof

    Source: US DOL Wage and Hour Division, Fact Sheet 43

    No meat slicers, saws or choppers.

    Kids under 18 cannot operate power-driven meat processing machines, such as meat slicers, saws and meat choppers

    Source: US DOL Wage and Hour Division, What jobs are off limits

    No power woodworking machines.

    Kids under 18 cannot operate power-driven woodworking machines, including chain saws, nailing machines and sanders.

    Source: US DOL Wage and Hour Division, What jobs are off limits

    No balers or trash compactors.

    Kids under 18 cannot operate balers, compactors (including trash compactors)

    Source: US DOL Wage and Hour Division, What jobs are off limits

    No forklifts and similar lifting equipment (page says most).

    Kids under 18 cannot operate, ride, or help use most power-driven hoisting apparatus

    Source: US DOL Wage and Hour Division, What jobs are off limits

    Bakery machines are off limits.

    Kids under 18 generally cannot operate, clean or repair power-driven bakery machines

    Source: US DOL Wage and Hour Division, What jobs are off limits

    Metal forming, punching and shearing machines are off limits.

    Kids under 18 cannot operate power-driven metal-working machines, including forming, punching and shearing machines.

    Source: US DOL Wage and Hour Division, What jobs are off limits

    Power saws are off limits.

    Kids under 18 cannot operate or help on power-driven circular saws, band saws, guillotine shears, chain saws

    Source: US DOL Wage and Hour Division, What jobs are off limits

    Roofing is off limits, even work on the ground.

    Kids under 18 cannot do most jobs in roofing operations, including work performed on the ground

    Source: US DOL Wage and Hour Division, What jobs are off limits

    Driving as a job is generally off limits.

    Kids under 18 generally cannot drive motor vehicles or work outside the vehicle as a driver's helper

    Source: US DOL Wage and Hour Division, What jobs are off limits

    Places that make or store explosives are generally off limits.

    Kids under 18 generally cannot work where explosives are manufactured or stored.

    Source: US DOL Wage and Hour Division, What jobs are off limits

    Demolition is off limits.

    Kids under 18 cannot do jobs in wrecking, demolition and ship-breaking operations.

    Source: US DOL Wage and Hour Division, What jobs are off limits

    Radioactive materials are off limits.

    Kids under 18 cannot do jobs where they are exposed to radioactive substances and ionizing radiation.

    Source: US DOL Wage and Hour Division, What jobs are off limits

    Trenches deeper than four feet are off limits.

    working in a trench more than four feet deep

    Source: US DOL Wage and Hour Division, What jobs are off limits

    Georgia: 14 and 15 year olds can only do jobs on the state's allowed list.

    Any job not specifically permitted for 14 and 15 year-olds, as listed below, is prohibited.

    Source: Georgia Department of Labor, Child Labor Hazardous Occupations

    Georgia says its hazardous list is based on federal law.

    The state definition of child labor hazardous occupations is based on federal law.

    Source: Georgia Department of Labor, Child Labor Hazardous Occupations

    New York says workers under 18 cannot be given jobs the law calls too dangerous.

    Workers under 18 years old cannot be hired or asked to do jobs the law says are too dangerous for young people.

    Source: New York State Department of Labor, Youth Worker Information Hub

    Illinois has its own list of hazardous jobs and workplaces for minors (the Illinois page frames its Child Labor Law as protecting workers under 16).

    There are 27 specific occupations, and 7 specific workplaces, which are considered "hazardous" or inappropriate for employment of minors.

    Source: Illinois Department of Labor, Child Labor Law FAQ

    Illinois compliance page example from its list.

    in construction work, including demolition and repair;

    Source: Illinois Department of Labor, Child Labor Law Compliance

    On a farm or ranch

    Farm work has its own federal rules. These farm rules are different from the non-farm lists on this page.

    • Ages 12 and 13: you may do non-hazardous farm jobs (jobs that are not on the dangerous list), only outside school hours. A parent must give written permission, unless the farm also employs a parent. Source: Agricultural Jobs - 12-13
    • Ages 14 and 15: you may work outside school hours, in jobs that are not on the dangerous list. Source: DOL Fact Sheet #40
    • Under 12: you may work only on a small farm that is exempt from the federal minimum wage, with a parent's consent, outside school hours, and only in non-hazardous jobs. Source: Agricultural Jobs - Under 12
    • A parent's own farm: children of any age may work in any job on a farm that a parent owns or runs. Source: DOL Fact Sheet #40

    3. Harassment and discrimination: what it is and how to report it

    The EEOC runs a youth section for teen workers. Its page says the laws protect you from being harassed by anyone in your workplace. Harassment is unwelcome behavior based on things like race, color, religion or sex. To break the law, it has to be severe (very serious) or happen often. The EEOC youth pages say to tell the person to stop if you feel comfortable, tell a manager, talk to a parent or another adult, keep records, and file with the EEOC. The EEOC deadline is generally 180 calendar days, and longer in some places. The EEOC pages we read do not state a minimum age. California's civil rights agency gives a three year deadline. New York's youth page says harassment based on race or ethnicity is against the law. The Georgia youth pages we read do not cover harassment. The Illinois Department of Labor page lists the number for the Illinois Department of Human Rights.

    What harassment is.

    Harassment is unwelcome conduct that is based on race, color, religion, sex

    Source: US EEOC, Harassment

    Protection covers anyone who harasses you at work, on the EEOC's youth page.

    The laws enforced by EEOC protect you from being harassed by anyone in your workplace.

    Source: US EEOC, Youth Harassment FAQs

    When it becomes illegal.

    the conduct must either be severe (meaning very serious) or pervasive (meaning that it occurred frequently)

    Source: US EEOC, Youth Harassment FAQs

    Step: tell the person to stop if you feel okay doing that.

    If you feel comfortable, tell the person harassing you to stop.

    Source: US EEOC, Tips for Youth @ Work

    Step: tell a manager.

    Report the behavior to your manager or another manager in your company.

    Source: US EEOC, Tips for Youth @ Work

    Step: talk to a trusted adult.

    Talk to your parents, teachers, or another adult.

    Source: US EEOC, Tips for Youth @ Work

    Step: keep records.

    Keep records including witness names, telephone numbers and addresses.

    Source: US EEOC, Tips for Youth @ Work

    Step: file with the EEOC.

    File a complaint with the EEOC.

    Source: US EEOC, Tips for Youth @ Work

    EEOC filing deadline.

    In general, you need to file a charge within 180 calendar days from the day the discrimination took place.

    Source: US EEOC, Time Limits For Filing A Charge

    The deadline can be longer.

    The 180 calendar day filing deadline is extended to 300 calendar days

    Source: US EEOC, Time Limits For Filing A Charge

    California: harassment is banned even at very small workplaces.

    Harassment is prohibited in all workplaces, even those with fewer than five employees.

    Source: California Civil Rights Department, Employment

    California: how to start a complaint.

    Start by filing an “Intake Form.”

    Source: California Civil Rights Department, Employment

    California: complaint deadline.

    must be filed within three years from the date an alleged discriminatory act occurred

    Source: California Civil Rights Department, Employment

    New York lists a discrimination free workplace as a right of youth workers.

    You have the right to a discrimination-free workplace

    Source: New York State Department of Labor, Youth Worker Information Hub

    New York says harassment because of race or ethnicity is against the law.

    It is against the law to be treated unfairly or harassed at your job because of your race or ethnicity

    Source: New York State Department of Labor, Youth Worker Information Hub

    New York: how to file a complaint with its labor department.

    You can file a complaint with the Department of Labor by filling out this form:

    Source: New York State Department of Labor, Youth Worker Information Hub

    4. If you get hurt: report it right away

    Tell your boss about any injury as soon as you can. Many states give a deadline after which you can lose benefits: 30 days in California, Georgia and New York, and 45 days in Illinois. Workers' compensation is the system that pays for care after a job injury. State by state: California's glossary says employee includes minors. New York's booklet for minors says it covers minors and pays double if a minor is hurt while working illegally. Georgia's Supervisor's Manual (July 2019) says employee includes minors. Illinois' handbook does not mention minors, but the Illinois Act says compensation goes up 50 percent for a worker under 16 who is illegally employed. The Georgia documents are dated July 2019 and the Illinois handbook is dated January 2013, so ask the state office if the rule has changed.

    California: tell your supervisor fast.

    Make sure your supervisor is notified of your injury as soon as possible.

    Source: California DIR, Division of Workers' Compensation, I was injured at work

    California: 30 day warning.

    If you don’t report your injury within 30 days, you could lose your right to receive workers’ compensation benefits.

    Source: California DIR, Division of Workers' Compensation, I was injured at work

    California: employer pays for medical care. (The page does not mention minors.)

    Medical care must be paid for by your employer if you get hurt on the job

    Source: California DIR, Division of Workers' Compensation, I was injured at work

    California: minors count as employees in the DWC glossary.

    The term employee includes undocumented workers and minors.

    Source: California DIR, Division of Workers' Compensation, Glossary

    Georgia: report right away and no later than 30 days (Employee Handbook, July 2019).

    You must report any accident immediately, but not later than 30 days after the accident, to your employer

    Source: Georgia State Board of Workers' Compensation, Employee Handbook

    Georgia FAQ: waiting too long can cost benefits.

    If you wait longer than 30 days, you may lose your benefits.

    Source: Georgia State Board of Workers' Compensation, Law FAQs

    Georgia: employers with three or more workers need coverage. The FAQ page does not mention minors.

    any business with three or more workers, including regular part-time workers

    Source: Georgia State Board of Workers' Compensation, Law FAQs

    Georgia: the Supervisor's Manual (July 2019) says employee includes minors.

    “Employee” generally means every person, including minors, in the service of another

    Source: Georgia State Board of Workers' Compensation, Supervisor's Manual (July 2019)

    New York: workers' compensation covers an injured minor.

    Workers’ Compensation is payable when a minor is injured in any way in the course of his or her employment.

    Source: New York State Department of Labor, Laws Governing the Employment of Minors (P882, 01/26)

    New York: report in writing right away.

    An injured youngster should report the accident immediately and in writing to his or her employer or foreman.

    Source: New York State Department of Labor, Laws Governing the Employment of Minors (P882, 01/26)

    New York: medical care is paid even with no lost time.

    Medical benefits are payable regardless of whether the employee has lost time from the job

    Source: New York State Department of Labor, Laws Governing the Employment of Minors (P882, 01/26)

    New York: double pay if the minor was working in violation of the law.

    his or her compensation is double the regular award

    Source: New York State Department of Labor, Laws Governing the Employment of Minors (P882, 01/26)

    New York: the employer pays the cost, not the worker.

    The cost of providing workers’ compensation insurance must be met entirely by the employer.

    Source: New York State Department of Labor, Laws Governing the Employment of Minors (P882, 01/26)

    New York youth page: a 30 day warning about telling your employer you were hurt.

    If you do not let your employer know you were injured at work within 30 days

    Source: New York State Department of Labor, Youth Worker Information Hub

    Illinois: 45 day outer limit to tell the employer. The handbook does not mention minors.

    Generally, the employee must notify the employer as soon as practicable, but no later than 45 days after the accident.

    Source: Illinois Workers' Compensation Commission, Handbook on Workers' Compensation

    Illinois Act: compensation rises 50 percent for a worker under 16 who is illegally employed.

    the injured employee is under 16 years of age at the time of the accident and is illegally employed

    Source: Illinois Workers' Compensation Act, Section 7(h)

    5. If your boss punishes you for speaking up (retaliation)

    Employers cannot punish you for using your safety or workplace rights. OSHA, the Department of Labor, the EEOC, California, New York and Illinois all say so on pages we read. If you think you were punished for speaking up, the OSHA page says deadlines vary and can be as short as 30 days, so act fast. The Georgia youth and workers' compensation pages we read do not address retaliation, so this page makes no Georgia claim about it.

    OSHA: safety rights come without punishment.

    Exercise your workplace safety rights without retaliation or discrimination.

    Source: US OSHA, Safe Work for Young Workers

    OSHA: you can file a whistleblower complaint.

    You have the right to file a whistleblower complaint with OSHA if you believe your employer retaliated against you

    Source: US OSHA, File a Complaint

    OSHA: the deadline can be short.

    Filing deadline is 30-180 days (deadlines vary with each statute)

    Source: US OSHA, File a Complaint

    US Department of Labor: no punishment for using your rights.

    An employer cannot retaliate against a worker for exercising their rights

    Source: US DOL Wage and Hour Division, YouthRules

    US Department of Labor: what an adverse action is.

    would dissuade a reasonable employee from raising a concern

    Source: US DOL Wage and Hour Division, Retaliation

    EEOC: you cannot be punished for reporting discrimination.

    you cannot legally be punished or fired for reporting job discrimination.

    Source: US EEOC, Tips for Youth @ Work

    California: no punishment for a job injury.

    It’s illegal for your employer to punish or fire you for having a job injury

    Source: California DIR, Division of Workers' Compensation, I was injured at work

    California Labor Commissioner youth brochure.

    Retaliation is illegal!

    Source: California Labor Commissioner's Office, Youth Advocate Program brochure

    New York: no firing or punishment for reporting a problem.

    Your employer cannot fire or punish you for reporting a problem at your workplace.

    Source: New York State Department of Labor, Youth Worker Information Hub

    Illinois Department of Labor: retaliation is against state law.

    It is against Illinois law for an employer to retaliate against a worker for exercising their rights

    Source: Illinois Department of Labor, File a Workplace Complaint

    Illinois workers' compensation: no firing or harassing for claiming benefits.

    harass, discharge, refuse to rehire, or discriminate in any way against an employee

    Source: Illinois Workers' Compensation Commission, Handbook on Workers' Compensation

    6. Who to call

    Federal and state offices answer questions by phone. OSHA is for unsafe work. The Wage and Hour Division is for child labor and pay. The EEOC is for harassment and discrimination. Each state office below handles child labor, workers' compensation, or harassment in that state. Every number is copied from the page named under it. Two Georgia pages gave different Wage and Hour numbers, so for Georgia we list only the Georgia Child Labor Section and the national Wage and Hour number. Calling an office is not legal advice from us, and we cannot say what an office will tell you.

    OSHA invites young workers to call with questions or to ask for an inspection.

    Call us on our toll-free number: 1-800-321-OSHA (6742) to get answers to your questions,

    Source: US OSHA, Safe Work for Young Workers

    Wage and Hour helpline hours and number.

    call our toll-free information and helpline, available 8 a.m. to 5 p.m. in your time zone, 1-866-4USWAGE (1-866-487-9243).

    Source: US DOL Wage and Hour Division, Fact Sheet 43

    DOL says complaints are free and private.

    Many investigations are initiated by complaints, which are free and confidential.

    Source: US DOL Wage and Hour Division, YouthRules

    EEOC number for questions.

    Call 1-800-669-4000

    Source: US EEOC, Tips for Youth @ Work

    California: Division of Workers' Compensation live help.

    Source: California DIR, Division of Workers' Compensation

    New York: help to file a complaint.

    If you need additional assistance to file your complaint, please call: 888-525-2267.

    Source: New York State Department of Labor, Youth Worker Information Hub
